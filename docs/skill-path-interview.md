# Skill Path Interview — Ryan's answers (raw)

Interviewed 2026-09-25. Ryan's words, organized by question, not rewritten.
Boundary: he named specific coaches in his answers but does NOT want anyone called
out publicly. App content teaches the technique, never the names. Jim Lofton is a
good friend and good coach; the app should not make him feel bashed.

## Q1: Where does the skill path actually start?

It really depends on the athlete. I will generally meet them where they are and my
goal is to find what they can do properly and challenge those limits without asking
too much from what they are physically capable of doing. Some athletes genuinely
aren't even ready for the most basic shapes. Ive had some athletes who have hit
recent growth spurts or surgeries or are still coming back early from an injury where
we have to scale and regress movements/exercises/body position holds to what they
can do, down much further than the average athlete. So the answer is really that it
depends. That said, there are ways to scale most things and I have those in the app
right now for a lot of exercises/shapes.

Working from the top down could be a good way of creating a skill path so we can
figure out where we would go if this isnt achieved yet, rather than from the bottom
up, because everybody's starting point may be a little different based off of their
physical capabilities, natural tuning of physics in movements, their air awareness,
movement foundations they have grown up building. Some kids like myself have had a
trampoline all of their life and develop a natural sense of how you move in the air
and how to control their bodies. Some kids have never been on a trampoline and
cannot hit a superman shape or even get close to hitting a lever shape.

## Q2: Rules you never break

I am sure there are many rules in my progression order I never break. I pretty much
always am adamant on athletes having a strong round off before even touching the
round off back handspring. I am also pretty adamant on athletes having a strong
round off series before working round off back handspring back tucks. I don't like
teaching fulls if an athlete doesnt have a strong layout, but i sometimes break that
rule if its close or have already gotten the skill. It messes up what i am trying to
get them though. Like most things, that depends on the athlete. I will never teach
a back walkover to an athlete that doesnt have a back bend. Ill never work round off
handspring layouts with an athlete that doesnt have a strong back tuck.

## Q3: Rules you break on purpose

I think that some conventional techniques may not be rules but some coaches treat
them as rules. I have had a handful of athletes over the years demonstrate useful
roundoffs while we are still struggling to control the cartwheel, and so i watched
an athlete get up to doing a decent round off series even though we still spend 5ish
mins on cartwheels at the beginning of our lesson. Some athletes can get tucked or
piked fulls without having a good layout. Some athletes can get round off back
handspring back tucks without a strong round off series since the feet behind problem
on their series is helpful for the back tuck. They generally have a hard time moving
past tucks into layout territory though.

Conventional technique "rules" can be broken and become technique hacks. Or maybe
its not that were breaking rules, and moreso just that the conventional techniques
are conventional because "thats how weve always done things" and some coach did it
this way and passed it on and unhelpful techniques got widespread like wildfire
amongst coaches who even achieved high level tumbling skills. Something i have
noticed as a coach who achieved high level tumbling myself, and had an easier time
acquiring skills than most people, is that the athletes who are most naturally
capable with learning and acquiring skills are the ones who have the hardest time
breaking skills down for people who dont have that natural ability for acquiring and
understanding skills to learn. I realized this when watching tumbling dee teach (i
dont want to call him out publicly for this), and even my own teaching, for a lot
of my earlier years of coaching with Levi and his dad. Its like we are thinking
"why is it so hard for this athlete to do this easy thing" rather than having this
magic way of breaking the skill into bite size pieces for athletes who aren't
naturally inclined. So i think that conventional techniques that aren't the best
ways to teach have been widespread amongst even high level athlete coaches. Which
becomes a problem because those high level athletes are sought after the most as
people to learn from. There are other high level athletes who went through all the
right progressions and learned how they work and the reasons behind them and learned
what techniques genuinely work even though they are unconventional, who become
amazing coaches because they learned the stuff that actually works despite being
unconventional. And there are some coaches who genuinely have a hard time
understanding how the unconventional techniques that genuinely work work. So Ive had
this battle of me wanting to be the best coach i can be, but also not feeling
comfortable making others feel wrong in what they do and how they teach. And so the
level of coaching i provide shows in the athletes i teach but is hard to preach an
influence seasoned coaches who havent got that understanding.

EYES DOWN is one of the biggest ones. I hear coaches always teaching round offs with
this big motion at the end of the round off for the rebound, focusing on getting the
arms and the eyes up over everything and then im just shaking my head in my mind.
Because its like, how do they not understand these basic physics concepts like
newtons third law, angular momentum and moment of inertia. The athlete needs to look
towards the ground and let their arms go down in front to get their chest up, not
the other way around. And instead of wasting time teaching rebounds, i teach them to
hold the right shape as they hit the floor learning to stand into a soft mat without
bending at the hips or knees to soften the impact. That is the thing about rebounds
for beginners especially: we dont want them to be taught as a bend and jump because
we completely defeat the purpose of a rebound which is supposed to recycle their
momentum. So keeping in mind all of the natural instincts that humans have that are
meant to protect them from potentially damaging things is important. We cant usually
get an athlete to stand directly into the landing of a round off on a hard surface
if they havent built that habit up onto a softer surface first, or trained the
punching motion at all without the skill in front of it. So i have watched tons of
high level athletes and high level coaches teach kids the wrong stuff because "thats
how its always been done" or a genuine misconception of real world physics. I teach
arms horizontal out of round offs and back handsprings because it helps the athlete
carry momentum backwards which is what they will be doing until they get a strong
series. And then after they get that strong series, i will teach them how to block
with feet behind on a soft mat with eyes forward and feet slightly behind to learn
how to stack their body and redirect their momentum to go up for blocking skills
like tucks, lays, fulls. It doesnt make sense to teach athletes eyes up arms up feet
way behind on beginner round offs that have never connected into the first BACKWARDS
skill (not upwards), a back handspring. The amount of times ive been made to feel
wrong for teaching this is insane. And then i go watch people like Anton Stolyar,
Elliot Helms (Cirque du Soleil artist/coach) and a million other coaches using
gymnastics and power tumbling progressions teach how i teach it. Yet i still hear
seasoned coaches who come from the rec and cheer tumbling world teaching kids to go
back and do their rebound (separated from the round off) and have arms up and eyes
up like thats the most important cue.

Teaching kids to round off into a lightning bolt shape creates habits I always have
to rework. Jim does this and i always have to rework the habits it creates with
athletes. I think weve come to a better place because now he also teaches it with
zombie as the landing shape so they learn both before coming up to me. Jim is a good
coach in most ways when it comes to teaching technique and teaching in shapes and we
share a lot of the same philosophies and are good friends at the gym so my hope is
that the app itself doesnt completely make him feel like his techniques are bashed
or trashed. He will really like this app because hes all about teaching in shapes.
Theres just a few things he sometimes does that I dont agree with and wish i could
clear up for him and other coaches who do the same thing.

Ive seen a lot of athletes get back tucks who cant do backward rolls.

One thing i do that might fall into rules that are ok to break, idk if its a rule,
but getting round off back handsprings independent before getting a standing
handspring independent. Some coaches might expect their athletes to have a standing
handspring before working a round off handspring, but what i have found is that with
more power, it becomes a lot easier to hold the right shapes on handsprings since
you have to open up a lot and increase moment of inertia a lot so the round off
helps to provide enough speed to allow the athlete to get the skill independently
with proper shapes and gradually work down to less power. That one is not the same
for everyone. Some athletes get standing first but ive found that the majority of
athletes i teach connections to get nice round off handsprings on the ground while
they have a standing still on the trampoline but not on the floor. And i think they
get stronger standing handsprings in the long run because they rep out handsprings
with proper shapes. Same goes for round off handspring tucks coming before standing
tucks. I think the best standing tucks come from properly trained running tucks. And
the athletes with running tucks that get stuck having a hard time creating habits
that move onto layouts are the ones who acquired the standing tuck.

I also have seen a lot of athletes with decent cartwheels still struggle with a good
handstand even on a wall. I dont think ive seen anyone hit a good layout that cant
do a proper hollow hold with arms up for at least 20 seconds. I say you want it for
a minute to expect that shape to hit in a layout on spring floor. And the tuck
should be super high and have enough flip to open and land with arms up and even
still we might not have enough angular momentum for a straight body to maintain its
shape on a layout.

We get round off handsprings from a passe fall and the standing handsprings get
stronger.

We get strong with straight arm handstand forward rolls and the athlete becomes
better at making unnatural feeling corrections, especially when it comes to resisting
the urge to close hips when a hollow shapes need to happen. Handstand to candle with
open hips translates to arch to hollow with open hips in the middle of a handspring
or on the set to pulling their flip shape on layouts. So some basics go a super long
way with creating habits and mental resilience for higher level tumbling. That is
something many athletes and coaches do not understand.

## Q4: Top of the path

The highest level skills I'd touch on are double backs and triple fulls.

## Q5: Stuck point — has RO-BHS, can't get the tuck

They need a round off series that meets these standards: feet in front 3 times,
accelerates without getting shorter on second and third handspring, carries power
long and fast, and athlete can stand into a strong hollow shape for a rebound out
of one, 2 or 3. They also need to get a good tuck on the trampoline from a passe
fall round off and/or as a stand alone tuck from bounces with arms up. They will be
able to get the skill if they have those things.

## Q6: Conventional cues replaced

I almost never say snap down. I dont think we should be teaching handstand snapdowns
to lower level athletes. I think this is one of the main places where bad rebound
habits start: body segmentation, bending and jumping, breaking shapes starts with
those for lower level athletes. I am not completely against it. Like theres a side
of my mind that doesnt want to be so rigid with perfect technique for lower level
athletes who we have to accept are in approximation phase but i also dont think that
means we should teach approximate techniques just because thats what we expect from
them. I think you can teach good habits and be patient with approximation at the
same time. Handstand snapdown rebounds when done correctly by higher level athletes
working blocking skills can be super beneficial.

"Drive your toes!" is another one i dont like to use. We want hips to come up too.
Driving toes alone often leads to piking in my experience.

"Dont be scared" usually doesnt help when athletes are struggling with fear.

"Get your chin in" on handsprings is not my favorite. "Cover the ears and look
through your hands" is much better imo.

"Chin to chest" i really have steered away from this over the last few years. For
handspring, tucks, really anything. I have never done a tumbling skill with my chin
on my chest and i think the idea of saying chin to chest to help an athlete get
their head in makes sense but ive found that the chin to chest cue is independent
from what the thoracic spine does. Neutral head position with chin slightly down and
a rounded thoracic spine is what i aim for for back tucks. I cannot even imagine
doing a back handspring with my chin on my chest. Coach Lain is one of my favorite
coaches and says it a lot but i think that is one big one where i disagree.

"Big rebound" out of round offs: id much rather a smaller amount of airtime on the
rebound post round off/handspring with the right shapes. I say "tight zombie at the
end" or cues for the zombie like arms in front or armpits in front of the toes or
stand into the springs.

"Sit" for handsprings is something Coach Lain has voiced reasons not to use and i
agree. "Bend" is better. Then "lean" is another Coach Lain has voiced reasons to not
use and i agree. Its semantics. "Fall" is better. We arent putting our weight on
another object so lean isnt it. We arent putting our weight onto an object for our
butt to sit on so "bend" makes more sense.

"Throw it" is another one Coach Lain has gone against. Its almost encouraging to
just recklessly throw our body into a skill. "Execute or perform or do" are better
ways to say it.

Thats a few, im sure there are more.

## Q7: Double back — what has to be in place

They need a back tuck at shoulder/head level. They should be able to over rotate a
back tuck to their backs on something at their shoulder level to realistically hit
the double back. Some tips and tricks are to increase rotation speed by using cowboy
tuck with knees apart. And when first trying it, to not trust their instinct. Over
rotating these is way better than under rotating them. On the first attempts, you
may think you know where you are and you will have the instinct to let out and
land. Trust me, your instinct is probably wrong and if you let out on the first
ones, youll under rotate and could neck it. Just stay in it and try to over flip
the first one even when you think youve done 2 flips.

## Q8: Triple full — what has to be in place

We need a high double with time to let out and slow the twist before landing. We
should finish the second twist not long after the half way point in the flip. The
more twist you want, the deeper the blocking angle needs to be. The more twist per
flip we want, the more height per flip we need. We cannot set and wait on the take
off for a triple full like we can for a single or some doubles. Dont stand up and
reach for the sky then twist. The twist arm will be moving down while the rebound
is pushing up. Adding a twist to the beginning of a double was easier for me than
adding a third twist to the end of a double. You need a strong double full with
plenty of time to open and land for this to work.

## Q7b/Q8b: What else helps (follow-up)

Also for a triple full, things that help are back 2.5 and front 2.5.

For a double, it helps to have a back 1.5 and a front 1.5. Or at least a front 1.5
goes a super long way. A high and straight full with time to open and land is
essential for a back double.

## Q9: Double full — what has to be in place

They need a strong back full straight with time to open up and land. And a front
rudi (1.5 twist).

## Q10: The shoulder-level back tuck — drill or just bigger

It doesnt have to be a listed skill, but a building block for double backs. It is
a back tuck high enough to where their back is at or above shoulder/head height.
Shoulder level isnt always enough. Over rotating it to their back on a shoulder
level mat is.

## Q11: Full twisting layout — the full picture

A strong layout is essential for a full twisting layout. The twist timing is
commonly taught between 11 and 1, but i think that between 10 and 12 might be
better since the half turn can spot the ground and allow the athlete to not feel
as lost. That still gives time to maximize the set before the twist and plenty of
time to stay hollow and land.

Teaching progression: lay to belly, half to back, and full to belly before having
the athlete attempt a full to feet. I sometimes use opposite side spotting to
preserve the set and bridge the gap between a spotted half and a spotted full.
